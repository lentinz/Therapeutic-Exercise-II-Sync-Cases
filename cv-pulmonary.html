About this model

This is a simplified, qualitative model of normal breathing, gas exchange, oxygen transport and the control of ventilation, at rest and during exercise. It shows direction and relative size of change, not values for a real person. Taylor, the typical person, rests at 12 breaths/min with a tidal volume of 500 mL (about 6 L/min of ventilation, 4.2 L/min reaching the alveoli), an alveolar PO₂ near 100 mmHg and an arterial saturation near 97%.

  • Exercise intensity is a percent of each person's own maximum (the same scale as the hemodynamics and electrophysiology models), so people are compared at the same relative effort. Heart rate and cardiac output come straight from the hemodynamics model.
  • Oxygen use (VO₂) comes from the Fick equation, VO₂ = cardiac output × arterial-venous O₂ difference. The maximal difference for each person (140, 150 and 160 mL/L for Drew, Taylor and Elliot) is an assumed, illustrative value, so VO₂max (about 35, 46 and 58 mL/kg/min) is illustrative too. A 70 kg body mass is assumed.
  • Ventilation rises almost linearly with VO₂ and then more steeply above the ventilatory threshold, as in the lecture figure. Maximal ventilation (80, 102 and 122 L/min) is tuned by hand within the lecture's range of 70 to 125 L/min. Resting ventilation here is about 6 L/min; the lecture quotes about 5 and the lab guide's case uses 7.
  • Dead space is set to a typical 150 mL per breath (see sources). It is not given on the lecture slides.
  • Control of breathing follows the lecture: a fast rise from central command and joint and muscle input at the start of exercise, then a slower rise as the chemical signals build. The split between the two is illustrative.
  • Gas exchange uses the alveolar gas equation. The oxyhemoglobin curve uses a published equation for hemoglobin saturation (see sources). Pressure at elevation comes from the barometric formula, and inspired PO₂ = FiO₂ × (PB − 47), the formula on the environmental considerations slides.
  • Elevation: acclimatization, altitude illness and how altitude limits performance are covered in the environmental considerations lecture and are not modeled here. Each workload stays at the same percent of the person's sea-level maximum, so a very high elevation with heavy exercise may show a warning that the workload could not really be sustained.
  • Pressures in the breathing cycle use the figures on the lecture slide at rest and scale up with exercise, in direction and relative size only.
  • Disease states (obstructive and restrictive disease, ventilation-perfusion mismatch, altitude illness) are not part of this version.
